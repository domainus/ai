# Cycle 1 adversarial audit — benchmark methodology

## Executive verdict

**FAIL for promotion to a verified optimizer win.** The strongest result survives review. On the recorded RTX 5080, Blackwell PS2 was faster than the repository's eager PS2 on all five large shapes, with closely matched polar error. The wall-time speedup was **1.42–2.10×**. At a fixed 409,600 synthetic training tokens and LR 0.02, the gain was **1.094×** in mean training throughput. Keep those results with their comparator and workload names.

Two findings change the interpretation of the other comparisons. I reproduced a stale warm-estimator cache hit after CUDA reused a freed tensor address. I also verified that returning BF16 updates sends Muon's FP32 parameter addition through per-tensor operations on this PyTorch build. The former can change the measured answer. The latter is a real cost of the tested integration that cannot be attributed to the polar kernel alone. The Tier-2 loss results do not establish a general optimizer ranking or loss parity.

## Methodological audit and statistical checks

I recomputed the summaries from the three requested JSON artifacts, inspected the benchmark entry points and all six test modules, and ran two focused probes. I did not rerun the full suite, training grid, or Apple benchmark. The source checkout was at `9db80e7` during review. The saved JSON does not bind the original runs to that commit.

### RTX 5080 timing

The large-shape comparison uses one seeded FP32 Gaussian input per shape, an FP64 SVD reference, and randomized method order within rounds. All 95 shape/method rows had finite quality outputs. For PS2, the five comparisons use the same tensor, coefficients, nominal FP16 work dtype, and FP32 public return dtype. Both clocks favor the Blackwell implementation.

| Input shape | PS2 eager wall, ms | PS2 Blackwell wall, ms | Wall speedup | Event speedup |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 512 × 2048 | 0.672 | 0.319 | 2.10× | 2.75× |
| 1024 × 4096 | 0.821 | 0.579 | 1.42× | 1.51× |
| 2048 × 8192 | 4.952 | 3.089 | 1.60× | 1.61× |
| 4096 × 4096 | 19.730 | 10.203 | 1.93× | 1.94× |
| 4 × 1024 × 4096 | 3.152 | 1.879 | 1.68× | 1.69× |

Speedups are ratios of unrounded medians. At 512 × 2048, eager and Blackwell wall IQRs were 0.037 and 0.013 ms. At 1024 × 4096, PS3 Blackwell took 0.510 ms by event and 0.582 ms by wall clock. S1-k3+4 Schatten-8 took 1.118 and 1.298 ms, and the warm S1-k3+4 took 1.573 and 1.837 ms. Even the fastest tested S1 took longer than PS3 on both clocks on every shape. The wall/event gap is consistent with launch or synchronization overhead, but it is not a measurement of CPU time. The two clocks measure different calls, and an event interval can contain device idle time while the host enqueues later kernels. At 4096 square the two clocks nearly agree. A host-only explanation for the S1 disadvantage fails there. S1 can still improve quality on that shape. Its Schatten-8 k4+5 relative error was 0.0735 versus 0.1268 for PS3.

The unified runner queues a zero-fill of twice L2 capacity before its start event on the current stream. For wall timing it synchronizes after the fill, starts the host clock, runs the method, then synchronizes again. The standalone Blackwell runner uses four times L2. The Tier-1 suite instead increments a twice-L2 byte buffer and synchronizes before starting its host clock. On this GPU these recipes touch 128, 256 and 128 MiB respectively. Stream ordering excludes the flush itself on the tested default device. None measures eviction effectiveness. A repeated zero-fill, a read-modify-write sweep and the cache state after LM backpropagation need separate labels. The head-to-head file stores the final load averages of 5.26, 3.76 and 4.05. The companion RTX run reports 16 logical CPUs. These observations neither prove saturation nor establish exclusive GPU access during either run. ### Tier-2 training

I found 72 unique optimizer/budget/LR/seed combinations, nine optimizers and zero flagged divergences. All 36 token-budget rows completed 200 steps of 2,048 tokens. Token counts, throughput arithmetic and all 18 best-LR mean validation losses reconcile with the raw rows. The separate 614,400-token, 300-step run produced the harvested matrices. The Cycle 1 LM comparison used 409,600 tokens or a 3-second training budget. The model had 19,208,704 parameters.

| Comparison at LR 0.02 | Seed 123 loss difference | Seed 124 loss difference |
|---|---:|---:|
| PS3 Blackwell minus eager PE, 200 steps | −0.01662 | +0.01338 |
| PS3 Blackwell minus Triton PE, 200 steps | +0.00471 | +0.00425 |
| PS3 Blackwell minus S1-k3+4 Schatten-8, 200 steps | +0.00129 | −0.00084 |
| PS3 Blackwell minus PS2 Blackwell, 3 seconds | −0.00432 | −0.01241 |

Lower loss is better. PS3 Blackwell had the lowest selected mean wall-budget loss, 2.86689, after 237 and 240 steps. Eager PE at the same LR completed 188 and 186 steps. The wall result answers how far these implementations trained in three seconds. At equal tokens the two seeds disagree on the PS3/eager PE ordering. Even two same-sign pairs give a minimum two-sided exact sign-test p-value of 0.5. The 12 evaluation batches in the source defaults share one model per trial. They cannot supply independent training replicates or establish equivalence.

The wall timer checks after a whole step, including `loss.item()`, which synchronizes each iteration. Across the 36 wall runs, observed overshoot was **0.55–14.61 ms**, median **6.49 ms**, at most **0.487%** of budget. This is small relative to the PS3/eager PE step gap. It still matters for close deadline rankings. Setup, warmup and final evaluation are outside the budget. Five disposable warmup steps in the source defaults exercise all four fixed matrix groups every step, including the 12-matrix gate/up group. This makes first-use tuning coverage plausible for these stateless entries. The artifact has no compile/autotune trace or warmup setting, and the runner always executes the token trial before the wall trial. There is no demonstration of steady clocks, equal cache history or cold-start cost. A future stateful method would also carry its estimator through warmup because `run` resets the model and optimizer but reuses the callable. ### Apple M5 Max

The saved run identifies MLX 0.32.5 on an M5 Max, FP32 arithmetic, seed 0, three Gaussian shapes and a CPU NumPy FP64 SVD. All 15 rows were finite, and all saved medians match the five raw samples. The code materializes lazy results with `mx.eval` and calls `mx.synchronize` before stopping the clock. Each method gets one warmup and then five consecutive samples in fixed method order on the same input. Cache state is uncontrolled. At 512 × 2048, PS2 ranged from 1.013 to 1.041 ms and PS3 from 0.995 to 1.104 ms. Their median difference was about 0.004 ms. Gram NS ranged from 0.999 to 2.165 ms there. The data support functional FP32 parity on the sampled inputs and descriptive native timings. They do not establish a close PS2/PS3 ranking, RTX-to-Apple speed ratios, or Apple mixed-precision training performance. ## Severity-ranked findings

**CRITICAL — A tensor address can select the wrong previous matrix's estimator.** The unified Tier-1 wrapper caches spectral state by `(previous.data_ptr(), tuple(previous.shape))`. It retains the estimator while the GPU allocation for the previous matrix may be released between cases. I exercised the actual wrapper on the RTX 5080 with FP16 S1-k3+4, cuBLAS Gram operations and seed 41. After one call on 48 × 96 matrices, I synchronized, released the previous CUDA tensor and allocated another of the same shape. The allocator returned the old address on the first attempt. With new contents there, the cached wrapper differed from a fresh wrapper on the identical current/previous pair by **0.11651 relative Frobenius norm**. Two fresh runs agreed exactly. The bug precedes the choice of Gram backend. Cycle 1 did not retain case-level state identities, so the number of affected archived rows is unknown. Rerun the warm-spectral Tier-1 rows before using them for promotion. The stateless PS2, PS3 and Schatten-8 comparisons bypass this cache. [Wrapper, lines 90–124](../research/polar_opt/run_cycle1_head_to_head.py#L90-L124)

**HIGH — Precision and update layout confound cross-family accuracy and throughput.** Eager PE casts to BF16 before normalization, performs the polynomial in BF16 and returns BF16. Triton PE also returns BF16 here, but normalizes in FP32, uses a distinct coefficient construction and fuses operations. PS2 and PS3 normalize in FP32 and work in FP16. Eager PS rounds its final product to FP16 before widening to the input dtype. Blackwell PS writes the last product directly to FP32 on these inputs. This preserves more final-product precision and avoids a cast pass. The same-variant comparison is a valid implementation comparison with a small rounding difference. Du and Su prescribe FP16 operands and FP32 accumulation for their reference implementation. FP16 is a reference design choice, not a mathematical requirement of the polynomial. The authors also supply QuACK/Triton backends and record fallbacks. No recorded local eager comparison measures speedup over those upstream optimized backends. [Eager implementations](../research/polar_opt/baselines.py) · [Triton PE](../research/polar_opt/kernels_blackwell.py) · [Blackwell PS](../research/polar_opt/polar_switch_blackwell.py) · [Pinned upstream design](https://github.com/zhehangdu/polar-switch/blob/6f382f1e4583804c62a2ebaa44917995bea8111f/polar_switch/README.md) · [Upstream dispatch](https://github.com/zhehangdu/polar-switch/blob/6f382f1e4583804c62a2ebaa44917995bea8111f/polar_switch/__init__.py#L134-L182)

Muon keeps FP32 master weights. Adding BF16 updates in place preserves the parameter dtype but cannot recover discarded update bits. I traced the actual CUDA foreach call on the same GPU and PyTorch 2.14.1+cu130, with six FP32 parameters and six unbound same-stride 64 × 64 updates. BF16 updates yielded one `aten::_foreach_add_` and six child `aten::add_` operations. FP32 updates yielded one foreach operation and zero child adds. Both produced the expected FP32 increments. PyTorch's fast-route guard also checks corresponding strides, so simply widening a transposed output may still miss it. This demonstrates a dispatch difference. Its share of the reported tokens/s and the direction or size of any validation-loss effect require an ablation. [Muon update](../research/polar_opt/harness_tier2_lm.py#L137-L151) · [Exact PyTorch CUDA dispatch](https://github.com/pytorch/pytorch/blob/5c4886908584029761b579af026dcfb627c84070/aten/src/ATen/native/cuda/ForeachBinaryOpList.cu#L194-L220)

**HIGH — Warm-spectral large-shape repeats give the estimator repeated access to the test matrix.** The runner primes a scaler on `0.95*G + 0.05*noise`, then updates it repeatedly on the same `G` during warmup, accuracy, event and wall passes. This synthetic neighbor has expected entrywise correlation about 0.9986 with G. For eligible circuits BF16 and FP16 closures also share the same mutable scaler. Accuracy uses the last warmup result. Timings need not use that same estimator state. The Tier-1 wrapper separately times cloning and copies only the vector, previous sigma, ratio and key. It omits generator position and pending refresh flags. A cold first step, one real previous/current transition and repeated application to a frozen matrix answer different questions. [Large-shape setup and quality](../research/polar_opt/run_cycle1_head_to_head.py#L145-L205) · [Estimator state](../research/polar_opt/spectral_scaling.py#L255-L290)

**HIGH — Tier-2 tests rapid learning of a modular motif and selects the LR on its evaluation set.** Independent train/evaluation RNG streams and matched initial weights make within-seed comparisons useful. Slot and phrase indices nevertheless expose a fixed eight-token grammar, with periodic topic resets and modular strides. There is no natural token-frequency tail or document-level language structure. An update that quickly learns periodic rules can look strong here. A difference that emerges during long training or cooling can disappear. The optimizer LR stays constant. AdamW uses fused CUDA on all parameters at 0.001 or 0.003. Muon uses 0.01 or 0.02 on hidden matrices and AdamW at 0.001 on the tied embedding and norms. Each gets two LR trials. **All nine token-budget winners are at the upper grid boundary.** AdamW at 0.003 scored 4.24546 and 4.86695 across seeds. Its better tested mean leaves its optimum unbracketed. Fixed method order and choosing/reporting the best mean on the same fixed evaluation stream add uncertainty. The step-11 heuristic divergence in the JSON is a hard-coded note, outside the 72 trials. [Generator and optimizer](../research/polar_opt/harness_tier2_lm.py#L96-L155) · [Grid and selection](../research/polar_opt/run_cycle1_tier2.py#L38-L119)

**HIGH — The harvested suite is development data and misses the actual Muon input.** Its 45 pairs come from three layers and three time windows of one seed-123 Jordan-Muon run on the same synthetic generator. The harvest saves pre-Nesterov EMA. Tier-2 orthogonalizes the Nesterov blend. Circuit search uses the harvested spectrum to set its search interval, computes calibration statistics on these pairs and evaluates on them. Reusing them in Tier 1 gives a development-set check, with correlated samples. It provides no independent estimate on a new training trajectory. [Harvest implementation](../research/polar_opt/harvest_gradients.py#L64-L104) · [Harvest record](../results/harvested_spectra.json) · [Search and calibration](../research/polar_opt/circuit_search.py#L539-L545)

**MEDIUM — The unified timing artifact drops the observations needed for replication.** It saves clock percentiles for large shapes and per-suite aggregates for Tier 1. It drops trial order, raw clock samples, per-case Tier-1 quality/timing, effective CLI settings, and run-level telemetry. Defaults of five large-shape warmups, 25 rounds and nine Tier-1 repeats are visible in code but unconfirmed for this artifact. The script silently omits harvested cases if the checkpoint is absent. All 45 were present in this run. A missing case or an exception should invalidate a required promotion gate. The suite's “diverged” condition uses nonfinites or maximum singular value greater than 2. A zero output can pass that guard. FP8×2 PE actually breached it in **11/117** cases, all in uniform/MP, despite zero nonfinite outputs. Exact rank-one errors also include arbitrary null-space completion in the reduced-SVD reference. Report supported-subspace error and leakage separately for that case. [Quality and summaries](../research/polar_opt/harness_tier1.py#L139-L162) · [Unified output](../research/polar_opt/run_cycle1_head_to_head.py#L325-L345)

**MEDIUM — The six test files have strong numerical coverage and specific integration gaps.**

| Test module | What it exercises | Important boundary |
|---|---|---|
| [Baselines](../tests/test_baselines.py) | Dtypes, input preservation, transpose equivariance, 4-D batches, degenerate spectra, coefficients and theoretical costs | No comparison of effective applied Muon updates or matched rounding between implementations |

Combine noncontiguous leading batches with output comparison for each member. The cache test uses the same values on one device and stream |
| [Blackwell PE kernels](../tests/test_kernels.py) | Strided SYRK, FP64 checks, odd sizes, 3-D batches, FP8 fallback/padding | Public Triton PE explicitly accepts ranks 2 and 3. Test rejection or implement a documented 4-D contract. Preserve the observed small-shape two-step FP8 failure in a targeted guard |
| [Spectral bounds](../tests/test_spectral_bounds.py) | Spikes, conditioning, scalar basins, CPU and CUDA paths, pairwise warm starts and 4-D input | Many trajectory checks skip if the file is missing. | [MLX](../tests/test_mlx.py) | Small tall/wide/square cases, independent SVD, zeros, CLI and recomputed median | Auto-backend permits NumPy on Linux. An MLX backend receipt is necessary for an Apple-specific gate |
| [Infographic](../tests/test_infographic.py) | CLI, markup, fixture charts and report labels | Provides reporting coverage only |

The Blackwell `_PLANS` key includes the tensor device, dtype, pointer alignment modulo 16 and integer launch arguments. A cached plan receives fresh pointers on each launch. Reusing an address does not by itself reuse old tensor data here. The demonstrated value-cache bug is in the benchmark wrapper. The compiled launcher obtains its default stream from the **current device**. No suite case intentionally warms a plan on one stream and reuses it on another, or targets CUDA device 1 while device 0 is current. These are unverified device/stream assumptions. The harnesses also lack direct tests for same-seed input pairing, warm-state reset, budget accounting and comparator selection. [Plan key and launch](../research/polar_opt/polar_switch_blackwell.py#L97-L119) · [PE rank contract](../research/polar_opt/kernels_blackwell.py#L191-L196)

**LOW — Apple sampling is too sparse for a close speed claim.** Each method occupies one contiguous timing block. A slow first block, thermal drift or allocator history can affect the whole block. The saved raw samples make the uncertainty visible. A claim about FP16/BF16 behavior or RTX performance needs a separate matched-precision experiment.

## Concrete remediation required before Cycle 2

1. **Invalidate and replace the warm-spectral Tier-1 rows.** Scope the reference estimator to a logical case. Build separate, complete state for each dtype and repeat outside the timed interval. Replay each previous/current pair once. Use a changed current matrix at each step of a streaming benchmark. Record state identity, refresh decisions and per-case output error. Require the freed-address regression above to agree with independent fresh-state runs.
2. **Isolate arithmetic from optimizer integration.** Keep the original native paths. Add comparisons with identical coefficients, normalization, work dtype, final rounding and contiguous FP32 update layout wherever both algorithms support them. Include conversion costs in the end-to-end clock. First hold update values fixed and vary only dtype/layout, then vary the computation precision. Record pre-add update error, applied FP32 parameter deltas, native/converted strides, foreach dispatch and time in the polar operation versus parameter update. For an upstream speed claim, run Du and Su's pinned optimized backend on the same inputs and record actual backend/fallback.
3. **Establish transfer on fresh data.** Retain the motif task as a smoke test. Collect actual Nesterov inputs from held-out seeds and real-text runs. Use disjoint training, tuning and final evaluation data on FineWeb or WikiText. Give AdamW and Muon equal tuning effort with family-appropriate grids that bracket their optima. Test a specified warmup/decay schedule on a longer horizon. Freeze settings and a meaningful loss-equivalence margin before fresh paired seeds. Size the confirmation run from pilot paired variance and report intervals for both loss and throughput.
4. **Make timing reproducible.** Save per-repeat event/wall samples, order, seeds, warmup, exact command, source revision, backend versions, GPU clock/power/temperature and host load during trials. Counterbalance run order across independent blocks. Report native warm-cache and cold-cache results, and verify eviction with an L2 hit-rate counter or a calibrated cache-sensitive probe. Profile launch gaps and foreach separately from timed trials. For wall-budget runs, keep the last completed step before the deadline or record checkpoints that bracket it. For Apple, interleave methods after stable warmup across multiple blocks and input seeds.
5. **Add focused promotion tests.** Exercise allocator reuse, separate streams with explicit dependencies, and a second GPU when available. Record hardware-dependent skips. Add a tiny deterministic Tier-2 test for identical data, independent state and whole-step budgets. Require the expected four Tier-1 suites, per-case quality limits, and explicit FP8 rejection or fallback where the saved artifact shows divergence. Verify the hard-coded step-11 failure from an archived trajectory before making a stability claim about its replacement.
